* Theorem:     Reduction to coordinates
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: thm:reduction-coordinates
:LECTURE_REF: 7.1.1
:END:

Let $\dim V = n$, $\dim W = m$, and $f : V \rightarrow W$. Choose bases $B_V \subseteq V$, $B_W \subseteq W$ and define $F : \mathbb{R}^n \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^m$ by

$$F := \chi_{B_W}^{-1} \circ f \circ \chi_{B_V}, \quad \text{or equivalently by} \quad f = \chi_{B_W} \circ F \circ \chi_{B_V}^{-1}.$$

Then $f$ is Frechet differentiable if and only if $F$ is Frechet differentiable. In that case, for $x = \chi_{B_V}^{-1} v$, we have

$$f'(v) = \chi_{B_W} \, F'(x) \, \chi_{B_V}^{-1}.$$

In contrast to linear algebra, we do not restrict to linear maps: if $f$ is nonlinear, then its coordinate representation $F : \mathbb{R}^n \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^m$ is nonlinear as well. Fréchet differentiable maps from $\mathbb{R}^n$ to $\mathbb{R}^m$ are often simply called /differentiable/.

#+begin_proof
Since the coordinate maps $\chi_{B_V}$ and $\chi_{B_W}$ are linear, their derivatives coincide with the maps themselves. Applying the chain rule, we obtain for $v \in V$ with $x = \chi_{B_V}^{-1} v$,

$$f'(v) = \chi_{B_W} \, F'(x) \, \chi_{B_V}^{-1}.$$
#+end_proof

* Definition:  Partial derivatives
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: def:partial-derivatives
:LECTURE_REF: 7.1.2
:END:

The function $f : \mathbb{R}^n \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$ is called partially differentiable at $x \in \mathbb{R}^n$ if, for each $j = 1, \ldots, n$, the limit

$$\partial_j f(x) := \lim_{h \rightarrow 0} \frac{f(x + h e_j) - f(x)}{h}$$

exists. The row vector

$$\operatorname{grad} f(x) := \left( \partial_1 f(x), \ldots, \partial_n f(x) \right)$$

is called the gradient of $f$ at $x$. Its transpose is a column vector and denoted by

$$\nabla f(x) = \operatorname{grad} f(x)^\top.$$

* Theorem:     Derivative and gradient in $\mathbb{R}^n$
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: thm:derivative-gradient-rn
:LECTURE_REF: 7.1.5
:END:

Let $f : \mathbb{R}^n \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$ be differentiable at $x \in \mathbb{R}^n$. Then $f$ is partially differentiable at $x$ and

$$f'(x) = \operatorname{grad} f(x).$$

In particular, $f'(x) e_j = \partial_j f(x)$, for $j = 1, \ldots, n$.

#+begin_proof
Since $f$ is differentiable at $x$,

$$f(x + h) = f(x) + f'(x) h + o(\|h\|) \quad (h \rightarrow 0).$$

Fix $j \in \{1, \ldots, n\}$ and set $h = t e_j$. Then, as $t \rightarrow 0$,

$$\frac{f(x + t e_j) - f(x)}{t} = \frac{f'(x)(t e_j)}{t} + \frac{o(\|t e_j\|)}{t} = f'(x) e_j + \frac{o(|t|)}{t}.$$

Hence the limit exists and equals $f'(x) e_j$, i.e.

$$\operatorname{grad} f(x) e_j = \partial_j f(x) = \lim_{t \rightarrow 0} \frac{f(x + t e_j) - f(x)}{t} = f'(x) e_j.$$

Thus, the two linear maps $\operatorname{grad} f(x)$ and $f'(x)$ coincide, because they are equal on the basis $\{e_1, \ldots, e_n\}$.
#+end_proof

* Theorem:     Continuity of partials implies differentiability
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: thm:continuity-partials-differentiability
:LECTURE_REF: 7.1.7
:END:

Let $U \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ be open and let $f : U \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$. If $f$ is partially differentiable in $U$ and all partial derivatives $\partial_1 f, \ldots, \partial_n f$ are continuous at $x \in U$, then $f$ is differentiable at $x$ and

$$f'(x) = \operatorname{grad} f(x).$$

#+begin_proof
Let $x \in U$ and $h = (h_1, \ldots, h_n) \in \mathbb{R}^n$ with $x + h \in U$. Define points

$$x^{(j)} := x + \sum_{i=1}^{j} h_i e_i, \quad j = 0, \ldots, n.$$

The telescope sum leads to

$$f(x + h) - f(x) = \sum_{j=1}^{n} \left( f(x^{(j)}) - f(x^{(j-1)}) \right).$$

Consider the one-dimensional functions

$$\varphi_j(t) := f(x^{(j-1)} + t e_j), \quad j = 1, \ldots, n,$$

defined for $t$ near 0. The partial differentiability yields

$$\varphi_j'(t) = \lim_{\delta \rightarrow 0} \frac{f(x^{(j-1)} + (t + \delta) e_j) - f(x^{(j-1)} + t e_j)}{\delta} = \partial_j f(x^{(j-1)} + t e_j).$$

By the mean value theorem, there exists $\theta_j \in (0, 1)$ such that

\begin{align*}
f(x^{(j)}) - f(x^{(j-1)}) &= \varphi_j(h_j) - \varphi_j(0) = \varphi_j'(\theta_j h_j) h_j \\
&= \partial_j f(x^{(j-1)} + \theta_j h_j e_j) h_j.
\end{align*}

We now obtain

$$f(x + h) - f(x) = \sum_{j=1}^{n} \partial_j f(x^{(j-1)} + \theta_j h_j e_j) h_j.$$

This implies

$$f(x + h) = f(x) + \sum_{j=1}^{n} \partial_j f(x) h_j + \varphi(h),$$

where the remainder $\varphi(h)$ is

\begin{align*}
\varphi(h) &= \sum_{j=1}^{n} \partial_j f(x^{(j-1)} + \theta_j h_j e_j) h_j - \sum_{j=1}^{n} \partial_j f(x) h_j \\
&= \sum_{j=1}^{n} \left( \partial_j f(x^{(j-1)} + \theta_j h_j e_j) - \partial_j f(x) \right) h_j.
\end{align*}

To verify $\varphi(h) / \|h\| \rightarrow 0$, we apply Cauchy-Schwartz in $\mathbb{R}^d$,

\begin{align*}
|\varphi(h)| &\le \sum_{j=1}^{n} \left| \left( \partial_j f(x^{(j-1)} + \theta_j h_j e_j) - \partial_j f(x) \right) h_j \right| \\
&\le \|h\| \left( \sum_{j=1}^{n} \left| \left( \partial_j f(x^{(j-1)} + \theta_j h_j e_j) \right) \right|^2 \right)^{1/2}.
\end{align*}

For $h \rightarrow 0$, we note that $x^{(j-1)} + \theta_j h_j e_j \rightarrow x$, so that continuity of $\partial_j f$ implies

$$\partial_j f(x^{(j-1)} + \theta_j h_j e_j) \rightarrow \partial_j f(x).$$

This leads to $\varphi(h) / \|h\| \rightarrow 0$ and hence $f$ is differentiable in $x$ with

$$f(x + h) = f(x) + \sum_{j=1}^{n} \partial_j f(x) h_j + \varphi(h) = f(x) + \operatorname{grad} f(x) h + \varphi(h).$$
#+end_proof

* Lemma:       Mixed partial derivatives commute
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: lem:mixed-partials-commute
:LECTURE_REF: 7.1.9
:END:

Let $U \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ be open and let $f : U \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$ be twice continuously partially differentiable. Then for all $i, j \in \{1, \ldots, n\}$ and all $x \in U$,

$$\partial_i \partial_j f(x) = \partial_j \partial_i f(x).$$

#+begin_proof
Fix $x \in U$ and indices $i, j$. Since $U$ is open, there exists $\epsilon > 0$ such that

$$x + s e_i + t e_j \in U \quad \text{for all } |s|, |t| < \epsilon.$$

Define the function $\varphi : \mathbb{R}^2 \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$ by

$$\varphi(s, t) := f(x + s e_i + t e_j).$$

Since $f$ is twice continuously partially differentiable, the function $\varphi$ has continuous second-order partial derivatives, and by the chain rule we have

$$\partial_1 \partial_2 \varphi(s, t) = \partial_i \partial_j f(x + s e_i + t e_j), \quad \partial_2 \partial_1 \varphi(s, t) = \partial_j \partial_i f(x + s e_i + t e_j).$$

It therefore suffices to show that

$$\partial_1 \partial_2 \varphi(0, 0) = \partial_2 \partial_1 \varphi(0, 0).$$

Using the one-dimensional mean value theorem twice, there are $\xi_s \in (0, s)$ and $\xi_t \in (0, t)$, such that

\begin{align*}
\underbrace{\varphi(s, t) - \varphi(0, t)}_{p(t)} - \left( \underbrace{\varphi(s, 0) - \varphi(0, 0)}_{p(0)} \right) &= \partial p(\xi_t) t = \partial_2 \left( \varphi(s, \xi_t) - \varphi(0, \xi_t) \right) t \\
&= \left( \partial_2 \varphi(s, \xi_t) - \partial_2 \varphi(0, \xi_t) \right) t \\
&= \partial_1 \partial_2 \varphi(\xi_s, \xi_t) s t.
\end{align*}

We repeat this process with interchanged roles of $s$ and $t$. There are $\eta_s \in (0, s)$ and $\eta_t \in (0, t)$, such that

\begin{align*}
\underbrace{\varphi(s, t) - \varphi(s, 0)}_{q(s)} - \left( \underbrace{\varphi(0, t) - \varphi(0, 0)}_{q(0)} \right) &= \partial q(\eta_s) s = \partial_1 \left( \varphi(\eta_s, t) - \varphi(\eta_s, 0) \right) s \\
&= \left( \partial_1 \varphi(\eta_s, t) - \partial_1 \varphi(\eta_s, 0) \right) s \\
&= \partial_2 \partial_1 \varphi(\eta_s, \eta_t) s t.
\end{align*}

We have shown that

$$\partial_1 \partial_2 \varphi(\xi_s, \xi_t) = \partial_2 \partial_1 \varphi(\eta_s, \eta_t).$$

The limit $(s, t) \rightarrow 0$ implies $(\xi_s, \xi_t), (\eta_s, \eta_t) \rightarrow 0$. The continuity of $\partial_i \partial_j f$ and $\partial_j \partial_i f$ yields continuity of $\partial_1 \partial_2 \varphi$ and $\partial_2 \partial_1 \varphi$ in 0, so that we arrive at

$$\partial_1 \partial_2 \varphi(0, 0) = \partial_2 \partial_1 \varphi(0, 0).$$
#+end_proof

* Definition:  Hessian matrix
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: def:hessian
:LECTURE_REF: 7.1.10
:END:

Let $U \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ be open and suppose that $f : U \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$ is twice continuously differentiable. The Hessian matrix of $f$ at $x \in U$ is defined by

$$H_f(x) := \left( \partial_i \partial_j f(x) \right)_{i, j = 1}^{n}.$$

*** DONE What does it mean for $U \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ to be open?

I think you forgot it a bit. Its simple.

A set $U \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ is *open* if every point has a "wiggle" room inside $U$. Formally: $\forall x \in U \, \exists \epsilon > 0$ such that

$$B_{\epsilon}(x) := \{ y \in \mathbb{R}^n : \|y - x\| < \epsilon \} \subseteq U$$

*** DONE Is this just a mere definition of a Hessian matrix with no deep meaning? Because I do not see one.

Yes, essentially. The definition is bookkeeping — $H_f(x)$ is /defined/ as the matrix of second partial derivatives.

Its meaning is unlocked by subsequent results that use it:

- Taylor's theorem (second-order expansion)
- Schwarz's theorem (symmetry)
- the second-derivative test (critical-point classification)
- the Fréchet second derivative (bilinear-form interpretation)

The matrix shape is chosen to anticipate its appearance as a quadratic form $h^\top H_f(x) h$ in Taylor expansions.

* Theorem:     Taylor formula in $\mathbb{R}^n$
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: thm:taylor-rn
:LECTURE_REF: 7.2.1
:END:

Let $U \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ be open, $k \in \mathbb{N}$, and let $f : U \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$ be $k$ times continuously differentiable. Then for every $x_0 \in U$,

$$f(x) = \sum_{|\alpha| \le k} \frac{\partial^\alpha f(x_0)}{\alpha!} (x - x_0)^\alpha + o(\|x - x_0\|^k) \quad (x \rightarrow x_0).$$

The Taylor polynomial,

$$x \mapsto \sum_{|\alpha| \le k} \frac{\partial^\alpha f(x_0)}{\alpha!} (x - x_0)^\alpha,$$

provides a polynomial approximation of $f$ near $x_0$.

*Remark 7.2.2* (Local approximation). /In data analysis, Taylor expansions are used to approximate complex models locally by simpler ones./

#+begin_proof
Set $h := x - x_0$ and assume $\|h\|$ is small enough so that the line segment $\{x_0 + t h : t \in [0, 1]\} \subseteq U$. Define the one-dimensional function

$$g : [0, 1] \rightarrow \mathbb{R}, \quad g(t) := f(x_0 + t h).$$

/Step 1: compute $g^{(j)}(0)$ in multi-index form./ For $j \in \{0, \ldots, k\}$ one proves by induction (using the chain rule and the commutation of mixed partial derivatives) that $g$ is $k$-times continuously differentiable and

$$g^{(j)}(t) = \sum_{|\alpha| = j} \frac{j!}{\alpha!} (\partial^\alpha f)(x_0 + t h) h^\alpha, \quad t \in [0, 1]. \quad (7.1)$$

In particular,

$$\frac{g^{(j)}(0)}{j!} = \sum_{|\alpha| = j} \frac{(\partial^\alpha f)(x_0)}{\alpha!} h^\alpha.$$

Summing over $j = 0, \ldots, k$ yields

$$\sum_{j=0}^{k} \frac{g^{(j)}(0)}{j!} = \sum_{|\alpha| \le k} \frac{(\partial^\alpha f)(x_0)}{\alpha!} h^\alpha,$$

which is exactly the Taylor polynomial of order $k$.

/Step 2: The univariate Taylor formula with integral remainder./ The univariate Taylor formula for $g$ yields

$$g(1) = \sum_{j=0}^{k-1} \frac{g^{(j)}(0)}{j!} + \int_0^1 \frac{(1-t)^{k-1}}{(k-1)!} g^{(k)}(t) \, dt.$$

We add and subtract $g^{(k)}(0)$ inside the integral and use $\int_0^1 (1-t)^{k-1} dt = 1/k$ to obtain

$$g(1) = \sum_{j=0}^{k} \frac{g^{(j)}(0)}{j!} + \int_0^1 \frac{(1-t)^{k-1}}{(k-1)!} \left( g^{(k)}(t) - g^{(k)}(0) \right) dt.$$

Since $g(1) = f(x)$, the remainder

$$R(h) := \int_0^1 \frac{(1-t)^{k-1}}{(k-1)!} \left( g^{(k)}(t) - g^{(k)}(0) \right) dt.$$

leads to the formula

$$f(x) = \sum_{j=0}^{k} \frac{g^{(j)}(0)}{j!} + R(h).$$

/Step 3: estimate the remainder./ We use the representation (7.1) for $j = k$ and derive

$$g^{(k)}(t) - g^{(k)}(0) = \sum_{|\alpha| = k} \frac{k!}{\alpha!} \left( (\partial^\alpha f)(x_0 + t h) - (\partial^\alpha f)(x_0) \right) h^\alpha.$$

Hence, with $|h^\alpha| \le \|h\|^{|\alpha|} = \|h\|^k$,

$$|g^{(k)}(t) - g^{(k)}(0)| \le \|h\|^k \max_{|\alpha| = k} \left| (\partial^\alpha f)(x_0 + t h) - (\partial^\alpha f)(x_0) \right| \sum_{|\alpha| = k} \frac{k!}{\alpha!}.$$

Thus, there is a constant $C > 0$ that depends only on $k$ and $n$ such that

$$|R(h)| \le C \|h\|^k \sup_{t \in [0, 1]} \max_{|\alpha| = k} \left| (\partial^\alpha f)(x_0 + t h) - (\partial^\alpha f)(x_0) \right|.$$

Since each $\partial^\alpha f$ with $|\alpha| = k$ is continuous at $x_0$, the function

$$\omega(h) := \sup_{t \in [0, 1]} \max_{|\alpha| = k} \left| (\partial^\alpha f)(x_0 + t h) - (\partial^\alpha f)(x_0) \right| \rightarrow 0$$

as $h \rightarrow 0$. Hence $R(h) = o(\|h\|^k)$.
#+end_proof

*** DONE What is this stange $|\alpha| \le k$ under the sum?

$\alpha$ is a multi-index: $\alpha = (\alpha_1, \ldots, \alpha_n) \in \mathbb{N}^n$, and $|\alpha| = a_1 + \ldots + a_n$.

It encodes mixed partial detivatives compactly:

\begin{align*}
\partial^{\alpha} f &= \partial_1^{\alpha_1} \ldots \partial_n^{\alpha_n}f \\
h^{\alpha} &= h^{\alpha_1} \ldots h^{\alpha_n} \\
\alpha! &= \alpha_1! \ldots \alpha_n! \\
\end{align*}

So $\sum_{|\alpha| \le k}$ means sum over all combinations of differentiation orders that total at most $k$. In $\mathbb{R}^2$ with $k=2$:

\begin{align*}
|\alpha| &= 0 : (0, 0) \rightarrow f \\
|\alpha| &= 1 : (1, 0), (0, 1) \rightarrow \partial_x f, \, \partial_y f \\
|\alpha| &= 2 : (2, 0), (1, 1), (0, 2) \rightarrow \partial_x^2 f, \, \partial_x \partial_y f, \, \partial_y^2 f \\
\end{align*}

\begin{align*}
T(x, y) = &f(x, y) \\
&+ (\partial_x f(x, y) \Delta x + \partial_y f(x, y) \Delta y) \\
&+ \frac{1}{2}(\partial_x^2 f(x, y) \Delta x^2 + 2\partial_x \partial_y f(x, y) \Delta x \Delta y + \partial_y^2 f(x, y) \Delta y^2) \\
\end{align*}

where the first-order term is the Jacobian acting on $h$:

\begin{align*}
\partial_x f(x, y) \Delta x + \partial_y f(x, y) \Delta y &=
\begin{bmatrix} \partial_x f(x, y) & \partial_y f(x, y) \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} \Delta x \\ \Delta y \end{bmatrix} \\
&= J_f \, h
\end{align*}

and the second-order term is the Hessian quadratic form in $h$:

\begin{align*}
\frac{1}{2}(\partial_x^2 f \, \Delta x^2 + 2 \partial_{xy} f \, \Delta x \Delta y + \partial_y^2 f \, \Delta y^2) &= \frac{1}{2}
\begin{bmatrix} \Delta x & \Delta y \end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix} \partial_x^2 f & \partial_{xy} f \\ \partial_{yx} f & \partial_y^2 f \end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix} \Delta x \\ \Delta y \end{bmatrix} \\
&= \frac{1}{2} h^{\top} H_f \, h
\end{align*}

* Theorem:     Second-order sufficient conditions for extrema
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: thm:second-order-sufficient-extrema
:LECTURE_REF: 7.2.5
:END:

Let $f : \mathbb{R}^n \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$ be twice continuously differentiable and let $x_0 \in \mathbb{R}^n$ be a critical point, i.e. $\operatorname{grad} f(x_0) = 0$.

1. If $H_f(x_0)$ is positive definite, then $x_0$ is a strict local minimum.

2. If $H_f(x_0)$ is negative definite, then $x_0$ is a strict local maximum.

3. If $H_f(x_0)$ is indefinite, then $x_0$ is a saddle point.

*Remark 7.2.6.* /If the Hessian $H_f(x_0)$ is positive semidefinite or negative semidefinite but not definite, the second-order test is inconclusive./

*Remark 7.2.7* (Local extrema). /In data-driven models, local extrema correspond to locally optimal parameter values where small changes do not improve the objective function./

#+begin_proof
Since $f$ is twice continuously differentiable, Taylor's formula of second order yields, for $h$ sufficiently small,

$$f(x_0 + h) = f(x_0) + \operatorname{grad} f(x_0) \cdot h + \frac{1}{2} h^\top H_f(x_0) h + o(\|h\|^2).$$

Because $x_0$ is a critical point, $\operatorname{grad} f(x_0) = 0$, and hence

$$f(x_0 + h) = f(x_0) + \frac{1}{2} h^\top H_f(x_0) h + \varphi(h),$$

where $\varphi(h) / \|h\|^2 \rightarrow 0$ for $h \rightarrow 0$.

1. The idea is to verify that $\frac{1}{2} h^\top H_f(x_0) h$ is positive and larger than $\varphi(h)$. We first define

   $$\mathbb{S}^{n-1} = \{x \in \mathbb{R}^n : \|x\| = 1\}.$$

   As the pre-image of the closed set $\{1\}$ of a continuous map $\|\cdot\|$, the set $\mathbb{S}^{n-1}$ is closed. It is also bounded. Heine-Borel extends from $\mathbb{R}$ to $\mathbb{R}^n$ and therefore $\mathbb{S}^{n-1}$ is compact. Since $h \mapsto \frac{1}{2} h^\top H_f(x_0) h$ is continuous, its image of $\mathbb{S}^{n-1}$ is compact. Therefore, it assumes its minimum, which must be a positive number since $H_f(x_0)$ is positive definite. We define

   $$\alpha := \min_{h \in \mathbb{S}^{n-1}} \frac{1}{2} h^\top H_f(x_0) h.$$

   For $h \in \mathbb{R}^n \setminus \{0\}$, we calculate

   $$\frac{1}{2} h^\top H_f(x_0) h = \frac{1}{2} \|h\| \frac{h^\top}{\|h\|} H_f(x_0) \frac{h}{\|h\|} \|h\| \ge \frac{1}{2} \alpha \|h\|^2.$$

   Since $\varphi(h) / \|h\|^2 \rightarrow 0$ for $h \rightarrow 0$, there is $\delta > 0$ such that

   $$\frac{|\varphi(h)|}{\|h\|^2} \le \frac{\alpha}{4}, \quad \forall \|h\| \le \delta.$$

   For $\|h\| \le \delta$, we obtain

   \begin{align*}
   f(x_0 + h) &= f(x_0) + \frac{1}{2} h^\top H_f(x_0) h + \varphi(h) \\
   &\ge f(x_0) + \frac{\alpha}{2} \|h\|^2 - \frac{\alpha}{4} \|h\|^2 \\
   &= f(x_0) + \frac{\alpha}{4} \|h\|^2.
   \end{align*}

   This shows that $x_0$ is a strict local minimum.

2. If $H_f(x_0)$ is negative definite, the same argument applied to $-f$ shows that $x_0$ is a strict local maximum.

3. Finally, if $H_f(x_0)$ is indefinite, there exist vectors $u, v \in \mathbb{R}^n$ such that

   $$u^\top H_f(x_0) u > 0 \quad \text{and} \quad v^\top H_f(x_0) v < 0.$$

   Choosing $x = x_0 + t u$ and $x = x_0 + t v$ with $t$ sufficiently small and using the Taylor expansion shows that $f(x) - f(x_0)$ takes both positive and negative values arbitrarily close to $x_0$. Hence $x_0$ is a saddle point.
#+end_proof

* Lemma:       Jensen's inequality
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: lem:jensen-inequality
:LECTURE_REF: 7.3.1
:END:

Let $f : \mathbb{R}^n \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$ be convex and $m \in \mathbb{N}$ with $m \ge 1$. Then

$$f(\sum_{i=1}^{m} \lambda_i x_i) \le \sum_{i=1}^{m} \lambda_i f(x_i), \quad x_i \in \mathbb{R}^n, \quad \lambda_i \in \left[ 0, 1 \right], \quad \sum_{i=1}^{m} \lambda_i = 1.$$

#+begin_proof
The case $m = 1$ is obvious and $m = 2$ is the definition of convexity. Assume that the claim holds for $m$. To verify the inequality for $m + 1$, we consider $\lambda_i \in \left[ 0, 1 \right]$ and $\sum_{i=1}^{m+1} \lambda_i = 1$. There must be one index $i$ such $\lambda_i \ne 0$. Without loss of generality, we may assume that $i = m + 1$, otherwise we may reorder. For $\lambda_{m+1} \ne 0$, we obtain

\begin{align*}
f(\sum_{i=1}^{m+1} \lambda_i x_i) &= f((1 - \lambda_{m+1}) \sum_{i=1}^{m} \frac{\lambda_i}{1 - \lambda_{m+1}} x_i + \lambda_{m+1} x_{m+1}) \\
&\le (1 - \lambda_{m+1}) f(\sum_{i=1}^{m} \frac{\lambda_i}{1 - \lambda_{m+1}} x_i) + \lambda_{m+1} f(x_{m+1}).
\end{align*}

The induction hypothesis for $m$ leads to

$$f(\sum_{i=1}^{m} \frac{\lambda_i}{1 - \lambda_{m+1}} x_i) \le \sum_{i=1}^{m} \frac{\lambda_i}{1 - \lambda_{m+1}} f(x_i),$$

because $\sum_{i=1}^{m} \frac{\lambda_i}{1 - \lambda_{m+1}} = \frac{1}{1 - \lambda_{m+1}} \sum_{i=1}^{m} \lambda_i = 1$. We therefore derive

\begin{align*}
f(\sum_{i=1}^{m+1} \lambda_i x_i) &\le (1 - \lambda_{m+1}) \sum_{i=1}^{m} \frac{\lambda_i}{1 - \lambda_{m+1}} f(x_i) + \lambda_{m+1} f(x_{m+1}) \\
&= \sum_{i=1}^{m+1} \lambda_i f(x_i).
\end{align*}
#+end_proof

* Theorem:     Gradient descent
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: thm:gradient-descent
:LECTURE_REF: 7.3.2
:END:

Let $f : \mathbb{R}^d \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$ be convex and differentiable and assume that

$$\| \nabla f(x) \| \le C \quad \text{for all } x \in \mathbb{R}^d$$

for some $C > 0$. Let $x^*$ be a minimizer of $f$. For an initial $x_0 \in \mathbb{R}^d$, consider gradient descent

$$x_{k+1} := x_k - \eta \, \nabla f(x_k)$$

with step size $\eta > 0$. Define the average iterate

$$\bar{x}_N := \frac{1}{N} \sum_{k=0}^{N-1} x_k.$$

Then for every $N \ge 1$,

$$|f(\bar{x}_N) - f(x^*)| \le \frac{\| x_0 - x^* \|^2}{2 \eta N} + \frac{\eta C^2}{2}.$$

In particular, with $\eta = \| x_0 - x^* \| / (C \sqrt{N})$,

$$|f(\bar{x}_N) - f(x^*)| \le N^{-\frac{1}{2}} \, C \| x_0 - x^* \|.$$

Thus, the error is bounded by $|f(\bar{x}_N) - f(x^*)|$ decays as $N^{-1/2}$.

#+begin_proof
Let $e_k := x_k - x^*$. Expanding the update gives

\begin{align*}
\| e_{k+1} \|^2 &= \| x_k - \eta \nabla f(x_k) - x^* \|^2 \\
&= \| e_k - \eta \nabla f(x_k) \|^2 \\
&= \| e_k \|^2 - 2 \eta \langle \nabla f(x_k), e_k \rangle + \eta^2 \| \nabla f(x_k) \|^2,
\end{align*}

and rearranging terms leads to

$$2 \eta \langle \nabla f(x_k), e_k \rangle = \| e_k \|^2 - \| e_{k+1} \|^2 + \eta^2 \| \nabla f(x_k) \|^2.$$

The first-order characterization of convexity in [[file:ch_06_analysis_in_normed_vector_space.org::#lem:first-order-convexity][Lemma 6.4.9]] yields

$$f(x_k) - f(x^*) \le \text{grad} \, f(x_k) \, (x_k - x^*) = \langle \nabla f(x_k), e_k \rangle.$$

The gradient bound, $\| \nabla f(x_k) \|^2 \le C^2$, leads to

$$2 \eta (f(x_k) - f(x^*)) \le \| e_k \|^2 - \| e_{k+1} \|^2 + \eta^2 C^2.$$

Summing from $k = 0$ to $N - 1$ telescopes:

\begin{align*}
2 \eta \sum_{k=0}^{N-1} (f(x_k) - f(x^*)) &\le \sum_{k=0}^{N-1} (\| e_k \|^2 - \| e_{k+1} \|^2) + N \eta^2 C^2 \\
&= \| e_0 \|^2 - \| e_{N+1} \|^2 + N \eta^2 C^2 \\
&\le \| e_0 \|^2 + N \eta^2 C^2.
\end{align*}

Divide by $2 \eta N$ and use convexity (Jensen) $f(\bar{x}_N) \le \frac{1}{N} \sum_{k=0}^{N-1} f(x_k)$ to conclude the claim.
#+end_proof

* Theorem:     Stochastic gradient descent for finite-sum problems
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: thm:stochastic-gradient-descent
:LECTURE_REF: 7.3.3
:END:

Let $f_1, \ldots, f_n : \mathbb{R}^d \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$ be convex and differentiable, and define

$$F(x) := \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} f_i(x).$$

Let $x^*$ be a minimizer of $f$. Assume that

$$\| \nabla f_i(x) \| \le C \quad \text{for all } x \in \mathbb{R}^d, \quad i = 1, \ldots, n,$$

for some $C > 0$. Let $(i_k)_{k\in\mathbb{N}}$ be independent uniformly distributed on $\{ 1, \ldots, n \}$, and define

$$x_{k+1} = x_k - \eta \, \nabla f_{i_k}(x_k),$$

with step size $\eta > 0$. Then for $\bar{x}_N := \frac{1}{N} \sum_{k=0}^{N-1} x_k$,

$$\mathbb{E}[F(\bar{x}_N)] - F(x^*) \le \frac{\| x_0 - x^* \|^2}{2 \eta N} + \frac{\eta C^2}{2}.$$

In particular, choosing $\eta = \| x_0 - x^* \| / (C \sqrt{N})$ yields

$$\mathbb{E}[F(\bar{x}_N)] - F(x^*) \le N^{-\frac{1}{2}} \, C \| x_0 - x^* \|.$$

*Proof idea.*

The only probabilistic input we use is the following elementary fact: if $i$ is independently, uniformly distributed on $\{ 1, \ldots, n \}$ and $\psi : \{ 1, \ldots, n \} \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^m$ is any function, then

$$\mathbb{E}_i[\psi(i)] = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{j=1}^{n} \psi(j).$$

In our setting, this implies

$$\mathbb{E}_{i_k}[\nabla f_{i_k}(x)] = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \nabla f_i(x) = \nabla F(x).$$

We now follow the proof of gradient descent and use the expectation at the appropriate locations. We omit the details.

Lipschitz is a /quantitative/ strengthening of continuity: a global linear bound $L$ on rate of change with explicit constant, rather than the qualitative $\varepsilon\delta$ relation.

Strict hierarchy:

Most continuous functions are /not/ Lipschitz:

- $\sqrt{x}$ fails on $[0,1]$ (slope blows up at $0$)

* Lemma:       Descent lemma
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: lem:descent-lemma
:LECTURE_REF: 7.3.5
:END:

Let $f : \mathbb{R}^d \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$ be differentiable and suppose that $\nabla f$ is L-Lipschitz continuous. Then

$$f(y) - f(x) \le \nabla f(x)^\top (y - x) + \frac{L}{2} \| y - x \|^2, \quad \forall x, y \in \mathbb{R}^d.$$

#+begin_proof
Define the path $\phi : \left[ 0, 1 \right] \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$ by

$$\phi(t) = f(x + t(y - x)).$$

The chain rule leads to

$$\phi'(t) = \nabla f(x + t(y - x))^\top (y - x).$$

The fundamental theorem of calculus and Cauchy-Schwartz yield

\begin{align*}
f(y) - f(x) &= \int_0^1 \phi'(t) \, dt \\
&= \int_0^1 \nabla f(x + t(y - x))^\top (y - x) \, dt \\
&= \nabla f(x)^\top (y - x) + \int_0^1 \left( \nabla f(x + t(y - x)) - \nabla f(x)^\top \right)^\top (y - x) \, dt \\
&\le \nabla f(x)^\top (y - x) + \int_0^1 \| \nabla f(x + t(y - x)) - \nabla f(x) \| \, \| y - x \| \, dt \\
&\le \nabla f(x)^\top (y - x) + \int_0^1 L \| t(y - x) \| \, \| y - x \| \, dt \\
&= \nabla f(x)^\top (y - x) + L \| y - x \|^2 \int_0^1 t \, dt \\
&= \nabla f(x)^\top (y - x) + \frac{L}{2} \| y - x \|^2.
\end{align*}
#+end_proof

* Theorem:     Gradient descent with Lipschitz gradient
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: thm:gradient-descent-lipschitz
:LECTURE_REF: 7.3.6
:END:

Let $f : \mathbb{R}^d \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$ be differentiable, bounded from below, so that $f(x) \ge r_0$ for all $x \in \mathbb{R}^d$. Suppose that $\nabla f$ is L-Lipschitz continuous. Then gradient descent

$$x_{k+1} = x_k - \eta \nabla f(x_k), \quad 0 < \eta < \frac{2}{L},$$

satisfies

$$\nabla f(x_k) \rightarrow 0.$$

#+begin_proof
By [[#lem:descent-lemma][Lemma 7.3.5]],

$$f(x_{k+1}) - f(x_k) \le \nabla f(x_k)^\top (x_{k+1} - x_k) + \frac{L}{2} \| x_{k+1} - x_k \|^2.$$

According to $x_{k+1} = x_k - \eta \nabla f(x_k)$, we derive

$$f(x_{k+1}) - f(x_k) \le -\eta \| \nabla f(x_k) \|^2 + \frac{L \eta^2}{2} \| \nabla f(x_k) \|^2.$$

We define $c := \eta(1 - \frac{L \eta}{2}) > 0$ and obtain

$$f(x_{k+1}) - f(x_k) \le -\eta(1 - \frac{L \eta}{2}) \| \nabla f(x_k) \|^2 = -c \| \nabla f(x_k) \|^2.$$

This implies $c \| \nabla f(x_k) \|^2 \le f(x_k) - f(x_{k+1})$ and summing from $k = 0$ to $N$ yields

$$c \sum_{k=0}^{N} \| \nabla f(x_k) \|^2 \le \sum_{k=0}^{N} (f(x_k) - f(x_{k+1})) = f(x_0) - f(x_{N+1}) \le f(x_0) - r_0.$$

Since this inequality holds for all $N \in \mathbb{N}$, we deduce that the series converges with

$$c \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \| \nabla f(x_k) \|^2 \le f(x_0) - r_0.$$

This implies that $\| \nabla f(x_k) \|^2 \rightarrow 0$.
#+end_proof

* Lemma:       Continuously differentiable implies Lipschitz on closed ball
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: lem:c1-implies-lipschitz-closed-ball
:LECTURE_REF: 7.4.1
:END:

Suppose $f : \mathbb{R}^n \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^m$ is continuously differentiable and $B_r \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ a closed ball. Then $f$ is $L$ Lipschitz continuous on $B_r$ with Lipschitz constant

$$L = \sup_{z \in B_r} \| f'(z) \|$$

#+begin_proof
For $x, y \in B_r$, define $g : \left[ 0, 1 \right] \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^m$ by $g(t) = f(y + t(x - y))$. The chain rule yields

$$g'(t) = f'(y + t(x - y))(x - y).$$

The fundamental theorem of calculus implies

$$f(x) - f(y) = g(1) - g(0) = \int_0^1 f'(y + t(x - y))(x - y) \, dt.$$

Taking norms on both sides leads to

$$\| f(x) - f(y) \| \le \int_0^1 \| f'(y + t(x - y)) \| \| (x - y) \| \, dt \le L \| x - y \|.$$
#+end_proof

* Theorem:     Quadratic convergence of Newton's method
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: thm:newton-quadratic-convergence
:LECTURE_REF: 7.4.2
:END:

Suppose $f : \mathbb{R}^n \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^n$ is twice continuously differentiable and $x^* \in \mathbb{R}^n$ such that $f(x^*) = 0$ and $f'(x^*)$ is invertible. Then there is $r > 0$ such that for all $x_0 \in B_r(x^*)$ the iterative sequence

$$x_{k+1} = x_k - f'(x_k)^{-1} f(x_k)$$

converges towards $x^*$ and there is $C > 0$ such that

$$\| x_{k+1} - x^* \| \le C \| x_k - x^* \|^2.$$

#+begin_proof
There is a radius $r > 0$ and a constant $M > 0$ such that

$$\| f'(x)^{-1} \| \le M, \qquad \forall x \in B_r(x^*).$$

(This will still be properly verified later.)

Put $e_k := x_k - x^*$ and again observe

$$\int_0^1 f'(x^* + t e_k) e_k \, dt = f(x_k) - f(x^*) = f(x_k).$$

We may add and subtract $f'(x_k) e_k$ to obtain

$$f(x_k) = f'(x_k) e_k + \int_0^1 (f'(x^* + t e_k) - f'(x_k)) e_k \, dt.$$

By subtracting $x^*$ from both sides of Newton's iteration, we get

\begin{align*}
e_{k+1} &= e_k - f'(x_k)^{-1} \left( f'(x_k) e_k + \int_0^1 (f'(x^* + t e_k) - f'(x_k)) e_k \, dt \right) \\
&= - f'(x_k)^{-1} \int_0^1 (f'(x^* + t e_k) - f'(x_k)) e_k \, dt.
\end{align*}

We may take norms on both sides and the triangle inequality still holds for the integration (as a limit of summations),

\begin{align*}
\| e_{k+1} \| &\le \| f'(x_k)^{-1} \| \int_0^1 \| f'(x^* + t e_k) - f'(x_k) \| \| e_k \| \, dt \\
&\le \| f'(x_k)^{-1} \| \int_0^1 L \| x^* + t e_k - x_k \| \| e_k \| \, dt \\
&= \| f'(x_k)^{-1} \| L \int_0^1 \| (t - 1) e_k \| \| e_k \| \, dt \\
&= \| f'(x_k)^{-1} \| L \| e_k \|^2 \int_0^1 (1 - t) \, dt \\
&= \| f'(x_k)^{-1} \| L \| e_k \|^2 \frac{1}{2} \\
&\le \frac{ML}{2} \| e_k \|^2.
\end{align*}

This is the quadratic error estimate with $C := \frac{ML}{2}$. To check for convergence, we decrease the radius of the ball centered at $x^*$. Choose a radius $\rho > 0$ such that $\rho \le r$ and $C\rho \le \theta < 1$. For $x_0 \in B_\rho(x^*)$, we have $\| e_0 \| \le \rho < 1$ and induction yields $\| e_k \| \le \rho$, so that the chain of inequalities leads to

$$\| e_{k+1} \| \le C \| e_k \|^2 \le C\rho \| e_k \| = \theta \| e_k \| \le \ldots \theta^{k+1} \| e_0 \| \rightarrow 0.$$

Thus, $x_k \rightarrow x^*$.
#+end_proof
